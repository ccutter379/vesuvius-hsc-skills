Answering HSC questions on the Cities of Vesuvius

This site does one thing: it shows you the thinking behind a strong HSC answer. Every question below is worked through in four moves — deconstruct the sources, deconstruct the question, write the response, then annotate every sentence to show what work it is doing and why it earns marks.

About the evidence in this site

Every source named here is a real source, with a real citation. Nothing has been invented. Where the exact wording of a translation could not be verified, the text is given as a clearly-labelled summary or standard rendering rather than inside quotation marks — check the precise wording against your own textbook and your study guide before you quote it in an assessment. Where scholars genuinely disagree (the eruption date, the function of the Building of Eumachia, how competitive the elections really were), the disagreement is presented as a disagreement, because recognising contested evidence is itself a top-band skill.

On the exam structure: Section I of the HSC Ancient History paper is the Cities of Vesuvius core and is worth 25 marks, made up of three or four parts, with one part worth 10 to 15 marks. The eight sets below are practice across that whole range of mark values (3 to 12) — they are not one 25-mark paper. Check the current paper and the marking guidelines at NESA before assuming any particular split.

Images: every photograph and painting here is public domain or Creative Commons licensed, credited in its caption and listed in full under Images: licences and credits. A few of them are records rather than remains — a watercolour copy of painted notices, an 1830 engraving, a modern copy of a mosaic — and the captions say so, because knowing the difference between evidence and a record of evidence is itself an examinable skill.

How to use it

  • Attempt first, reveal second. Every model answer is hidden behind a Reveal the model answer toggle. Write your own version before you open it — reading a model answer you have not attempted teaches almost nothing.
  • Read the margin notes. The orange notes beside each sentence explain what that sentence is doing. That is the actual lesson; the answer itself is just the demonstration. Use the Marker’s notes button in the top bar to hide them and re-read the answer clean.
  • Do the Your turn question. Each set ends with a parallel question on the same skill using different evidence, and a self-marking checklist instead of an answer.
  • Print the sets you want as handouts. Everything expands automatically when printed and the images convert to greyscale for photocopying.

The four-move method

Every set on this site follows the same four moves. They are in this order for a reason: students lose most of their marks by writing before they have read.

1

Deconstruct the sources

Work through every source with the same five-part frame, whether it is a letter, an inscription or a photograph of a building:

FrameThe question you ask
OriginWho made it, where, when? Is it contemporary with what it describes?
Nature & purposeWhat kind of thing is it, and what was it for? An epitaph advertises; a sewer deposit advertises nothing.
ContentWhat does it actually show or say — stated plainly, before you interpret it?
ValueWhat can a historian learn from it that they could not learn elsewhere?
LimitationsWhat does it leave out, distort, or over-represent? Whose voice is missing?

Looking at an archaeological image is a skill in itself. Do it in this order: (1) what is the first thing you notice; (2) what is it made of, and how well made is it — cheap or costly, mass-produced or commissioned; (3) what is its archaeological context, that is, where exactly was it found and what was found with it. The third question is the one students skip, and it is usually where the marks are. A lararium fresco in a tavern means something different from the same fresco in a mansion.

2

Deconstruct the question

Before writing, isolate five things: the directive verb and its NESA meaning; the topic; the city or cities named (answer only those); the source instruction (“using Source A and Source B” is compulsory, not a suggestion); and the mark value, which tells you how long to write.

Translating marks into length

A rough working guide, not a rule: 3–4 marks — two to four sentences, no introduction, no conclusion. 5–8 marks — a tight paragraph or two, opening with a claim, closing with a limitation or judgement. 10–15 marks — a thesis sentence, three or four themed paragraphs, a conclusion. The commonest self-inflicted wound in this topic is writing a 12-mark answer to a 4-mark question and running out of time in Section IV.

3

Write the response — then read the annotations

The marking unit of this topic is a triple: one named piece of evidence + what it reveals + one limitation or debate. If a sentence contains none of those three, ask what it is for. NESA’s band language runs limited → basic → sound → thorough → comprehensive; the jump from “sound” to “thorough” is almost always the addition of evaluation, not the addition of more content.

4

Your turn

A parallel question on the same skill with different sources, and a checklist to mark yourself against. No model answer — that is the point.

Question set 1 · Short answer

The geographical setting and natural features of Campania 3 marks

Syllabus: the geographical setting and natural features of Campania (Survey). Skill focus: writing short, and using both sources when there is no room to spare.

1

Deconstruct the sources

Source A — written

Strabo describes the country around Vesuvius as covered with excellent fields, except at the summit, which is largely flat, unfruitful and ash-coloured, with porous rock that looks as though it has been eaten out by fire. He infers that the mountain was once alight and had craters of fire, which went out when the fuel was exhausted — and he connects this volcanic past to the exceptional fertility of the surrounding plain.

Strabo, Geography Book 5. Written by a Greek geographer working at Rome in the Augustan period, roughly 7 BC–AD 18 — some seventy years before the eruption.

Summary of Strabo’s argument, not a verbatim translation. Check your own edition before quoting.

Source B — archaeological
FrameSource A — StraboSource B — Bacchus fresco
OriginGreek geographer at Rome, c. 7 BC–AD 18; not an eyewitness of the eruption.Painted in a household shrine in a Pompeian house, before AD 79.
Nature & purposeSystematic geographical description for an educated readership.Religious image for domestic worship — but also a celebration of the local product.
ContentFertile fields; burnt, sterile summit; fire-eaten rock; inference of extinct volcanic activity.The wine god literally clothed in the region’s crop, beside a vine-covered mountain.
ValueShows that ancient observers linked the volcano to the soil’s richness — the reason people farmed a dangerous plain.Shows Pompeians themselves associating the mountain with wine and prosperity, in their own homes.
LimitationsWritten decades earlier and far away; treats Vesuvius as spent, not dormant. Praise-writing about Campania was a literary convention.The mountain is not labelled — the identification as Vesuvius is a reasonable reading, not a certainty. Religious imagery is idealised.
2

Deconstruct the question

Using Source A and Source B, outline TWO natural features of Campania that contributed to the prosperity of Pompeii. 3 marks

VerbOutline — sketch in general terms; indicate the main features. You are not being asked to explain at length, evaluate, or judge.
ScopeTWO features. Not one, not four. The word “natural” excludes things people built — the forum is not a natural feature.
CitiesPompeii only. Do not spend a sentence on Herculaneum.
SourcesBoth are compulsory. At 3 marks you cannot afford a sentence that uses neither.
Marks → lengthRoughly a mark per feature plus a mark for linking them to prosperity through the sources. Two to three sentences. No introduction.
What the marker wantsTwo clearly named features, each explicitly connected to wealth, with both sources drawn on.
How students lose marks(1) Naming features but never linking them to prosperity — the question’s actual demand. (2) Describing the sources instead of using them (“Source B is a fresco which shows…”). (3) Writing a 200-word answer and losing time they need later.
3

The modelled response

Reveal the model answer

Campania’s volcanic soils were the first natural feature underpinning Pompeii’s prosperity. Source A shows that Strabo already connected the burnt, cindery summit of Vesuvius to the excellent fields below, and Source B shows Pompeians celebrating the result inside their own homes: Bacchus, the wine god, clothed entirely in grapes beside a vine-covered mountain.

What this move doesNames the feature in the first six words, then uses both sources on that single feature. At 3 marks there is no room for a sentence that carries no source.

These soils yielded three or four crops a year, including the vines behind the local Vesuvinum wine.

What this move doesOne short sentence of own knowledge converts the image into an economic fact. “Outline” does not forbid precision — it forbids length.

Second, the Sarno River and the sheltered Bay of Naples gave Pompeii access to both river and sea routes, so the wine, oil and garum produced from these soils and the bay’s rich fisheries could be sold well beyond Campania — the labelled garum jars of Aulus Umbricius Scaurus turn up across the western Mediterranean.

What this move doesThe second feature, and the explicit link the verb demands: the feature produced trade, and trade produced wealth. Scaurus is one named example, not a list — enough to show range without padding.

Total: 89 words. That is the right size for 3 marks.

Your turn

Using a source of your choice and your own knowledge, outline TWO ways the land around Vesuvius was used for commercial agriculture. 3 marks

  • Have I named exactly two uses, and are both commercial rather than subsistence?
  • Have I used the source, rather than describing it?
  • Have I added one named example from my own knowledge — Jashemski’s root casts of the vineyard near the amphitheatre, or the wine presses and buried dolia at Villa Regina, Boscoreale?
  • Is it under 100 words?
Question set 2 · Short answer

The eruption of AD 79 and its impact 4 marks

Syllabus: the eruption of AD 79 and its impact on Pompeii and Herculaneum (Survey). Skill focus: comparing along an axis rather than writing two separate descriptions.

1

Deconstruct the sources

Source A — written

Watching from Misenum, about thirty kilometres across the bay, Pliny the Younger describes a cloud rising from the mountain in the shape of an umbrella pine — a tall trunk spreading out into branches at the top. Ash and pumice fell, daylight failed, the ground shook repeatedly and the sea was sucked back from the shore. His uncle, Pliny the Elder, commander of the fleet at Misenum, sailed towards the eruption to observe it and then to rescue those trapped, landed at Stabiae, and died on the beach the following morning.

Pliny the Younger, Letters 6.16 and 6.20, written to the historian Tacitus at Rome around AD 106 — roughly twenty-five years after the eruption. Pliny was seventeen at the time. He never names Pompeii or Herculaneum.

Summary of the two letters, not a verbatim translation. The phrase “umbrella pine” is the standard rendering of his simile.

Source B — archaeological

Reading Source B

First thing you notice: the chambers are open to the sea, not part of a house — and the ground in front of them is where the beach used to be. Material and context: these are vaulted stone boat sheds built into the terrace wall; the coastline is now hundreds of metres inland because the eruption pushed it seaward, so the whole town sits below and behind the modern shore. What the context adds that the structure alone cannot: the roughly 300 skeletons excavated inside these chambers prove the direction of flight. Herculaneum’s people did not die at home in their beds — they ran to the sea, and something caught them there. That is why the discovery changed the interpretation of a town that had for two centuries looked almost empty of victims.

2

Deconstruct the question

Using Source A and Source B and your own knowledge, compare the effects of the eruption of AD 79 on Pompeii and Herculaneum. 4 marks

VerbCompare — show how things are similar and/or different. This is a structural instruction: your answer must be organised around points of difference, not around two towns in turn.
Scope“Effects” means both the burial of the cities and the deaths of the people — and, at the top of the range, the different evidence each burial produced.
SourcesBoth, plus own knowledge. Note that Source A does not distinguish the two towns at all — so own knowledge is doing real work here, not decorating.
Marks → lengthAround four sentences. Roughly: one on Pompeii’s mechanism, one on Herculaneum’s, one on the human and evidential consequence, one of evaluation.
What the marker wantsA clear axis of comparison — fall phase versus surge phase — with correct detail attached to the right town, and both sources genuinely used.
How students lose marks(1) Writing two disconnected descriptions with no comparative words. (2) Swapping the towns’ details — the pumice fell on Pompeii, not Herculaneum. (3) Claiming Pliny watched Pompeii being destroyed; he was thirty kilometres away and never names it.
3

The modelled response

Reveal the model answer

Pompeii was buried by the very phase Pliny describes in Source A. The umbrella-pine column drove south-east on the prevailing wind and dropped pumice, or lapilli, on Pompeii at roughly fifteen centimetres an hour to a depth of nearly three metres; roofs collapsed under the accumulating weight, which caused the first deaths, while most residents fled during those twelve to eighteen hours of falling stone.

What this move doesAnchors the comparison in Source A rather than mentioning it decoratively: the column Pliny saw is the mechanism that buried Pompeii. Note the causal chain — wind, fall rate, depth, roof collapse, deaths.

Herculaneum, upwind to the west, received almost no pumice at all and was instead destroyed in the second, Pelean phase, when the column collapsed: pyroclastic surges one and two struck the town at around 400 to 500 degrees Celsius and buried it under some twenty to twenty-three metres of material that set hard as tuff.

What this move doesThe comparison runs along one axis — fall versus surge — signalled by “instead”. This is what turns two descriptions into a comparison. Sigurdsson’s numbered surges give the sequence precision.

Source B shows what that meant for people: about three hundred skeletons were excavated from these vaulted boat chambers on Herculaneum’s beach, where those who fled seaward died instantly of thermal shock. The contrast in the surviving evidence follows directly from the contrast in burial — Herculaneum’s airless, damp deposit carbonised organic material and preserved skeletons, while at Pompeii bodies decayed inside the loose ash and left cavities, which Fiorelli began filling with plaster in 1863.

What this move doesUses Source B for the sophisticated half of the comparison: the two eruptions produced two different archives. Casts versus skeletons, ash versus tuff, decay versus carbonisation. This is the sentence that separates a strong answer from a competent one.

Pliny is invaluable but limited here: writing roughly twenty-five years later from thirty kilometres away, he never names either town, so the differing fate of the two is reconstructed from stratigraphy — Sigurdsson and Carey’s work from 1982 — and from the skeletal evidence, rather than from any eyewitness.

What this move doesOne sentence of evaluation, and only one. It also does something clever: it explains why own knowledge was necessary in this answer, which shows the marker you understand the limits of the source you were given.

Your turn — representations

Using the source above and your own knowledge, identify ONE nineteenth-century and ONE twenty-first-century representation of the eruption, and outline one difference in how each presents it. 4 marks

  • Have I named specific works? Karl Briullov’s The Last Day of Pompeii (1830–33) or Bulwer-Lytton’s novel (1834); the film Pompeii (2014) or a virtual-reality tour.
  • Have I identified each one’s purpose and audience, not just its content?
  • Have I named a concrete difference — divine punishment of a decadent city versus digitally modelled volcanology with an invented harbour tsunami?
  • Have I treated representations as evidence about their own era, which is what this dot point is actually testing?
Question set 3 · Middle range

The economy: industries, trade and occupations 5 marks

Syllabus: the economy: role of the forum, trade, commerce, industries, occupations (Focus of study). Skill focus: getting several distinct revelations out of one short source.

1

Deconstruct the sources

Source A — written (an inscription on an object)

A round carbonised loaf of bread from Herculaneum, stamped before baking: “made by Celer, slave of Quintus Granius Verus”.

Carbonised stamped loaf, from the house of Quintus Granius Verus, Herculaneum. Preserved by carbonisation in the AD 79 deposit.

Standard rendering of the stamp. The object is simultaneously a written source and an archaeological one — worth saying so in an answer.

Source B — archaeological
FrameSource A — the loaf of CelerSource B — the bakery
OriginMade in Herculaneum on the day of the eruption; stamped by or for the household of Q. Granius Verus.Pompeii, in use in AD 79, excavated as standing remains. The building has been consolidated by modern restorers.
Nature & purposeA commercial product, marked for identification — a maker’s stamp or quality mark, not a document about society.Industrial plant: the fixed capital of a business.
ContentA worker’s name, his legal status as a slave, and his owner’s name.Grain mills of imported lava stone and a large wood-fired oven, integrated on one site.
ValueDocuments branding, production for sale beyond the household, and the enslaved workforce — all in one object, incidentally rather than for display.Shows the scale and technology of the trade: milling and baking were done under one roof.
LimitationsA single object. Tells us nothing about output, price or Celer’s conditions.Capacity is visible; turnover, profit and wages are not. The building has been consolidated by modern restorers.
2

Deconstruct the question

Using Source A and Source B and your own knowledge, explain what the evidence reveals about the organisation of the baking industry in Pompeii and Herculaneum. 5 marks

VerbExplain — make relationships evident; provide why and how. An explain answer that contains no because, so or which meant is usually a describe answer in disguise.
Scope“Organisation” is the key word — how the industry was structured: its plant, its scale, its labour, its market. Not a description of what bread was like.
CitiesBoth. The two sources conveniently give you one each.
Marks → lengthA tight paragraph, four or five sentences. Open with a claim, close with a limitation.
What the marker wantsEach source used for something the other cannot supply, own knowledge providing scale, and one evaluative sentence.
How students lose marks(1) Opening with “Source B is a photograph of a bakery” — a wasted sentence at 5 marks. (2) Retelling how Roman bread was made instead of answering about organisation. (3) Getting all the way through without one limitation.
3

The modelled response

Reveal the model answer

Baking in both towns was organised as a specialised, capitalised trade rather than a domestic chore. Source B shows the plant that required: four hourglass mills of imported lava stone, turned by donkeys or by slaves, standing beside a large wood-fired oven — grain-milling and baking integrated on a single commercial site.

What this move doesOpens with the claim that answers “organisation”, then evidences it. Never open a 5-mark answer by identifying what the source is; the marker can see that.

Its scale is measurable from own knowledge: more than thirty pistrina have been identified at Pompeii, and eighty-one carbonised loaves were still inside the oven of the Bakery of Modestus at the moment of the eruption.

What this move doesOwn knowledge supplies what a photograph cannot — how many bakeries, and how much one oven held. This is exactly the source-plus-own-knowledge pairing the instruction demands.

Source A adds the labour and the commerce. Because the loaf is stamped “made by Celer, slave of Quintus Granius Verus”, a single object documents three things at once: bread was marked for identification or quality, which implies it left the household and entered a market; production was organised under a named owner; and the person who actually did the work was enslaved.

What this move doesThree distinct revelations from one very short text, explicitly counted. High-value questions reward density, not length — and “which implies” is the explain verb doing its job.

The organisation of labour was therefore mixed: free proprietors owning the fixed capital, enslaved workers like Celer doing the milling and baking, and the finished product sold on into a town where many houses had no kitchen.

What this move doesSynthesises both sources into one structural statement. Notice it also explains demand — the industry was large because kitchens were scarce, not because Romans liked bread.

Neither source records output, prices or wages, however: the mills reveal capacity rather than turnover and Celer’s working conditions are unknowable, which is why the wider question of whether Pompeii’s workshops served a local market or a regional export trade remains contested between Moeller, who argued for substantial industry, and Jongman, who saw a consumer town living off its hinterland.

What this move doesLimitation plus named debate in one sentence. At 5 marks this is the difference between “sound” and “thorough” in the NESA band language.

Your turn

Using the source above and your own knowledge, explain what carbonised food remains reveal about the food trades of Pompeii and Herculaneum. 5 marks

  • Have I opened with a claim rather than a description of the photograph?
  • Have I explained why this evidence survives — carbonisation in the airless, damp Herculaneum deposit, and charring inside sealed Pompeian ovens?
  • Have I used the scoring into portions as evidence of production for sale rather than for one household?
  • Have I supplied scale from own knowledge — over thirty pistrina at Pompeii, and eighty-one loaves left in the oven of the Bakery of Modestus?

Question set 4 · Middle range

Everyday life: food and dining 6 marks

Syllabus: everyday life: housing, leisure activities, food and dining, clothing, health, baths, water supply, sanitation (Focus of study). Skill focus: arguing that one type of evidence is stronger than another, and saying why.

1

Deconstruct the sources

Source A — written

A painted sign from a drinking establishment at Herculaneum, known as the Ad Cucumas sign, shows four wine jugs (cucumae) of graded size with a price marked beside each — advertising four grades of wine at four different prices.

Painted shop sign, Herculaneum, in use in AD 79. One of very few surviving Roman priced drink lists.

Described from its content rather than transcribed, because the numerals and their exact reading are best checked in your own textbook.

Source B — archaeological

Reading Source B

First thing you notice: the counter faces the street, and the jar is sunk into it — this is a serving hatch for passers-by, not a kitchen for a family. Material and workmanship: the masonry is plain and functional, but the shrine behind it is expensively painted, which tells you the proprietor had money and wanted it seen. Context: the bar stands on the Via dell’Abbondanza, one of Pompeii’s busiest commercial streets; a coin hoard was found in one of its dolia. What the context adds: two arguments the objects alone cannot give you. First, food retail was a street-front business dependent on passing trade. Second, this single image is also evidence for religion in the workplace — the same genius, Lares and serpent that appear in domestic shrines are here in a shop, which is worth remembering for question set 7.

2

Deconstruct the question

Using Source A and Source B and your own knowledge, explain what the evidence reveals about food and drink in Pompeii and Herculaneum. 6 marks

VerbExplain — again, causes and relationships. Here the best explanation is about why the evidence is shaped the way it is as well as what it says.
ScopeBoth food and drink. Both cities. Aim across the social range — the 6-mark value buys you the elite as well as the ordinary.
Marks → lengthFive or six sentences, or two short paragraphs.
What the marker wantsSpecific named evidence, an awareness that different evidence types have different reliability, and at least one limitation.
How students lose marks(1) Listing foods with no source attached. (2) Relying on Petronius’ feast of Trimalchio as though satire were documentation. (3) Ignoring drink entirely when Source A is about drink. (4) Repeating the old claim that Romans were malnourished, which the evidence contradicts.
3

The modelled response

Reveal the model answer

Food and drink in both towns were substantially bought rather than cooked at home, and the diet they supported was more varied and considerably better than older assumptions about the Roman poor allowed. Sources A and B are both evidence of the same commercial system: Source A prices four grades of wine, and Source B is the counter it would have been sold across.

What this move doesOpens with a claim about the system, then states in one sentence how the two sources relate to each other. Examiners reward an answer that treats the sources as connected evidence rather than two separate exhibits.

What people actually ate is known most directly from waste rather than from writing. The Cardo V sewer beneath Insula Orientalis II at Herculaneum, excavated by the Herculaneum Conservation Project, preserved the refuse of the ordinary tenants in the flats above: fish, sea urchin, figs, olives and grapes — and black peppercorns, an imported luxury reaching non-elite tables.

What this move doesNames the site, the excavators, and the detail that breaks the assumption. Sewer deposits record what people excreted rather than what they claimed to eat, which makes them the least distorted dietary evidence in the topic — and pepper in an apartment block is worth more than a longer list of figs.

The retail side is documented by both sources together. The Ad Cucumas sign in Source A advertises four grades of wine at four prices, which implies a graded and competitive market for drink; Source B supplies the premises, with its dolia sunk into a counter opening straight onto the Via dell’Abbondanza. This existed on a large scale — roughly 150 to 160 thermopolia at Pompeii — because a great many homes in both towns had no kitchen at all.

What this move doesPairs the written source with the archaeological one on a single point, then explains the cause: no kitchens, therefore a food-retail economy. That causal step is what the verb “explain” is asking for.

At the top of the social scale the pattern inverts: wealthy households dined at home in the triclinium, reclining nine to three couches, served with vessels like the 118-piece silver service found in the House of the Menander.

What this move doesOne sentence covers the elite. Six marks buys you the social range, but do not let the mansions crowd out the sewer — the ordinary evidence is rarer and more interesting to a marker.

Each source nonetheless samples narrowly. The sewer deposit represents a single insula over perhaps twenty years; carbonisation preserves dry bakeable foods far better than meat or greens, skewing what survives; and the once-standard claim that counter dolia like the one in Source B held hot stew is now doubted, since porous unglazed jars suited dry goods better than liquids.

What this move doesThree limitations in one sentence, the last of them a correction to what many textbooks still say. Markers notice students who know the field has moved.

Your turn — water supply and sanitation

Using the sources above and your own knowledge, explain what the evidence reveals about water supply and sanitation in Pompeii and Herculaneum. 6 marks

  • Have I traced the system: the Augustan Aqua Augusta branch, the castellum aquae at the Porta Vesuvio, fourteen water towers, roughly forty public fountains, lead fistulae to subscribing houses?
  • Have I used the sharpest contrast between the towns — Pompeii’s streets doubling as storm drains, hence stepping stones, against Herculaneum’s proper underground Cardo V sewer, which meant it needed none?
  • Have I explained the consequence for daily life rather than just describing pipes?
  • Have I noted that both towns were mid-repair in AD 79 after the earthquake, so this is a snapshot of disruption rather than normality?
  • Have I flagged that the health effects of lead pipes are contested rather than asserting lead poisoning?
Question set 5 · Middle range

Local political life 7 marks

Syllabus: local political life: decuriones, magistrates, comitium (Focus of study). Skill focus: an assess question — leading with a judgement and proving it.

1

Deconstruct the sources

Source A — written

“The fruit-sellers together with Helvius Vestalis urge you to elect Marcus Holconius Priscus duumvir with judicial power.”

A painted electoral notice (programma) on a street facade at Pompeii. About 2,800 such notices survive, most relating to the elections of AD 79.

Standard rendering of a typical programma. The formula — supporters, candidate, office — is fixed; the supporters vary.

Source B — archaeological

Reading Source B

First thing you notice: the size and quality of the lettering — this is professional signwriting, not scratched graffiti. That matters, because programmata were commissioned and paid for, so they measure organised support rather than spontaneous popularity. Context: the wall is a shopfront on the Via dell’Abbondanza, one of the busiest commercial streets in the town. What the context adds: election campaigning happened on trading premises along the main thoroughfares, borrowing other people’s walls — including, elsewhere on the same street, the frontage of the bar run by the innkeeper Asellina, a woman who could not vote but publicly backed candidates.

One further point, and a genuinely examinable one: this image is a nineteenth-century watercolour record of the notices, not a photograph of the wall as it survives now. It is therefore evidence about the evidence — invaluable, because the paint has faded badly since, but one further step removed from AD 79. Recognising the difference between evidence and a record of evidence is exactly the kind of source criticism that earns marks.

2

Deconstruct the question

Using Source A and Source B and your own knowledge, assess the usefulness of painted electoral notices as evidence for local political life in Pompeii. 7 marks

VerbAssess — make a judgement of value or quality. An assess answer with no judgement sentence cannot reach the top bands, however much content it contains.
Scope“Usefulness” means useful for what. Be specific: highly useful for the mechanics of campaigning, useless for voting outcomes.
CitiesPompeii — but the comparison with Herculaneum, which has almost no programmata, is a legitimate and powerful limitation.
Marks → lengthTwo solid paragraphs, or five to six developed sentences. Judgement first, judgement again at the end in different words.
What the marker wantsQuantity of the evidence, a specimen quoted briefly, the supporter networks, the formulaic limitation, at least one named historian, and a verdict.
How students lose marks(1) Describing what programmata say without ever judging their usefulness. (2) Transcribing whole notices to fill space. (3) Treating painted enthusiasm as evidence of votes won. (4) Naming no scholars, when this dot point has a genuine and well-known debate. (5) Forgetting that much of what we can read was recorded by nineteenth-century copyists before the paint faded.
3

The modelled response

Reveal the model answer

Painted electoral notices are the richest surviving evidence for electoral culture anywhere in the Roman world, and they are outstandingly useful for the mechanics and the social reach of campaigning — but they measure visibility rather than votes, and they survive in usable quantity at Pompeii alone.

What this move doesA 7-mark assess needs a judgement in the first sentence, with a built-in “but”. Everything that follows now has a job: proving one half or the other of this sentence.

Their first value is sheer quantity: roughly 2,800 programmata survive on Pompeian facades, professionally painted by named scriptores and mostly relating to the elections of AD 79 — a density of political documentation no other Roman town can match. Source A shows the standard formula of supporters, candidate and office: the fruit-sellers, together with Helvius Vestalis, urging the election of Marcus Holconius Priscus as duumvir with judicial power.

What this move doesScale first, then a single short specimen. Notice the quotation is trimmed to the phrase that carries the formula — never transcribe a whole source when a clause will do.

Their real analytical value lies in those supporter lists, because they reveal a politics reaching far beyond the male electorate. Trade groups campaign as blocs — fruit-sellers, muleteers, goldsmiths, fishermen; the millers and “all the worshippers of Isis” back Gnaeus Helvius Sabinus; and women who could not vote endorse candidates publicly, like the innkeeper Asellina and her staff. Source B shows how physically dominant this was: an entire shopfront on the Via dell’Abbondanza given over to notices, so that campaigning was inseparable from the commercial streetscape.

What this move doesUses Source B for what a text alone cannot prove — the scale and placement of campaigning on trading premises. The image supplies the find-spot argument rather than decorating the answer.

Their limitations are equally clear. Egyptian religion at Pompeii was neither a marginal foreign import nor merely decorative fashion. Even where the verb is a low-order one such as outline or describe, Year 12 answers are expected to use precise terminology, named evidence and correct citation. Where the verb is assess, evaluate, analyse or to what extent, the marker is looking for contestability: what is debated, who debates it, and what remains uncertain. Saying honestly that the evidence cannot settle a question is a strength at this level, not a hedge.

Integrating source evidence into a paragraph

Most lost marks in this topic come from one habit: writing about the sources instead of arguing with them. The three pairs below are the same content written twice.

Weak

Source B is a photograph of a bakery in Pompeii. It shows some mills and an oven. This tells us that the Romans made bread. There were many bakeries in Pompeii.

Four sentences, no claim, no argument. It identifies and describes the source, which the marker can already do, and the final sentence has no evidence attached.

Strong

Baking was a capitalised trade rather than a household task: the bakery of Popidius Priscus in Source B integrates lava-stone mills with a large wood-fired oven on one commercial site, and with more than thirty pistrina at Pompeii the industry clearly served a market, not a family.

One sentence. It opens with a claim, uses the source as proof of that claim, and adds own knowledge that supplies the scale the photograph cannot.

Weak

Pliny the Younger wrote two letters about the eruption. He said the cloud looked like an umbrella pine. However, Pliny is a biased source because he wrote it later, so it is not very reliable.

The evaluation is generic and slightly wrong. “Biased” is doing no work, “not very reliable” is unsupported, and in fact volcanology has confirmed Pliny’s physical details — the real limitations lie elsewhere.

Strong

Pliny’s umbrella-pine simile is precise enough that volcanologists still call this eruption type Plinian; his limitations are therefore not accuracy but distance and purpose — he watched from thirty kilometres away, wrote some twenty-five years later to memorialise his uncle, and never names either city.

Concedes the source’s strength before naming its actual weaknesses, and each weakness is specific and evidenced. This is what evaluation looks like at Stage 6.

Weak

Women in Pompeii had no rights. They could not vote. Eumachia was a priestess who built a big building, which shows some women were important.

The first and third sentences contradict each other and the contradiction is never addressed. “Important” and “big” are not analytical words.

Strong

Legal exclusion and public prominence coexisted: Eumachia held no office and could not vote, yet as sacerdos publica she funded the largest building on the forum’s east side and the fullers honoured her with a statue inside it — female authority ran through property and priesthood rather than through the cursus honorum.

Names the tension, resolves it with a distinction, and supports both halves with specific evidence. The last clause is the analytical payoff.

The pattern underneath all three

Claim → source as proof → own knowledge for scale or context → one limitation or debate. That four-part unit is the mark-scoring shape of this topic. If a sentence does none of those four jobs, cut it and use the time on Section IV.

Common mistakes, and the fix

The mistakeThe fix
“Pliny the Younger watched Pompeii being destroyed.”He watched from Misenum, about thirty kilometres away, and never names Pompeii or Herculaneum. Write that he described the eruption, not the cities’ destruction.
Treating 24 August AD 79 as certain fact.Present it as the traditional date from medieval manuscripts of Pliny and from Cassius Dio, then give the autumn evidence — the charcoal graffito of 17 October found in Region V in 2018, autumn produce among the carbonised food, sealed wine dolia — and note that most scholars now favour late October while certainty is impossible.
Answering a 4-mark question at 12-mark length.Convert marks into sentences before you write. Time lost in Section I cannot be recovered in Section IV.
Structuring an extended response source by source.Structure by theme. Each themed paragraph should pair a provided source with your own named evidence.
“Source A is a written source which tells us…”Delete the sentence. Open with the claim and let the source prove it.
Asserting that Pompeii was a great industrial exporter.Name the debate: Moeller argued for a substantial wool-export industry, Jongman for a consumer town living off its hinterland. Say that the commerce was diverse and energetic but its scale is contested.
Claiming the counter dolia in bars held hot stew.The porous unglazed jars suited dry goods. “Fast-food bar” is fine; confident menus are not.
“Only the old, the weak, women and children died.”Estelle Lazer’s Pompeian sample is a normal cross-section of ages and sexes. Survival was a matter of timing and luck, not fitness.
“Freedmen took over a city in decline.”That is Maiuri’s dated crisis theory. Wallace-Hadrill showed grand and humble houses interleaved in the same insulae, with commerce and residence always mixed. Cite the counter-argument before you judge.
Claiming you can identify enslaved people’s skeletons.Legal status leaves no skeletal signature. Argue from documents, architecture and objects instead — and say openly that the gap exists.
Treating Petronius’ Trimalchio as evidence for Pompeii.It is Roman satire written for elite amusement. Usable as evidence of attitudes towards freedmen; not usable as documentation of Pompeian society.
Asserting that Christians lived at Pompeii.The evidence — a lost charcoal graffito and the Herculaneum wall “cross” that may be a shelf bracket’s shadow — is weak and disputed. Say unproven.
“The eruption preserved the cities, so they are safe.”Excavation un-preserves: everything exposed begins to decay. The modern battle is conservation, not discovery.
Writing that Italy neglects Pompeii.Use the 2010 Schola Armaturarum collapse as the crisis and the Great Pompeii Project and Herculaneum Conservation Project as the partial turnaround. A balanced evaluation scores higher than blame.
Calling the Building of Eumachia “the fullers’ headquarters”.Its function is genuinely debated — guild hall, cloth market or multi-purpose portico. Say so; it is a neat example of the limits of archaeological inference.
Ending an assess answer with a summary.End with a judgement. Restate your thesis in fresh words and commit to a position.

Three things to do in every answer

  1. Read the question twice and note which city or cities it names. Answer only those.
  2. Per paragraph: one named piece of evidence, what it reveals, one limitation. That triple is the mark-scoring unit of this topic.
  3. Flag debates honestly. The eruption date, the function of the Eumachia building, how competitive the elections were, whether Christians were present, the health effects of lead pipes. Showing where knowledge is uncertain is a top-band skill, not a weakness.

About the evidence, and what to check

How the sources here were handled

  • Every source is real and cited. Ancient writers are cited by author, work and book or letter number; archaeological evidence by site, building and, where relevant, its CIL inscription number.
  • No invented quotations. Where the precise wording of a translation could not be verified, the text is given as a labelled summary or a standard rendering, outside quotation marks. Short formulaic texts — the programma in set 5, the loaf stamp in set 3 — follow the renderings in your own study guide.
  • Contested points are marked as contested, including the eruption date, the function of the Building of Eumachia, how competitive Pompeii’s elections really were, whether Christians were present, and the health effects of lead pipes.
  • Figures that vary between studies — surge temperatures, visitor numbers, population estimates — are given as ranges or as approximations rather than as single precise values.

Worth checking against your own materials

  • The exact wording of any source you intend to quote in an assessment task, especially Pliny’s umbrella-pine simile, the Julia Felix rental notice (CIL IV 1136) and the Popidius Celsinus dedication.
  • The numerals on the Ad Cucumas sign in set 4. The sign advertises four grades of wine at four prices; the precise prices are best taken from your textbook.
  • The current Section I structure and mark allocation. Section I is 25 marks with three or four parts, one worth 10 to 15 marks, but the split changes from year to year — check the most recent paper and marking guidelines.
  • Population and casualty figures. Roughly 1,150 bodies recovered at Pompeii and about 300 skeletons in the Herculaneum boat chambers are reasonably firm; total death tolls and city populations are estimates.
  • Two images are records rather than remains, and the captions say so: the electoral notices in set 5 are a nineteenth-century watercolour copy, and the Street of the Tombs in set 6 is an 1830 engraving. The Alexander Mosaic in set 7 is photographed as the modern copy laid in its original position; the original is in Naples.
